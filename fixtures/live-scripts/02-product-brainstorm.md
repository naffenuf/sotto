# Script 2: Brainstorming a shared-garden tool

**People:** Alex and Sam. **Setting:** Two people are exploring a lightweight tool for community gardens. Read your own lines naturally; you can paraphrase. Start Sotto before beginning and respond naturally if it speaks. Adjust later lines if the conversation takes a different direction. Pauses are opportunities for participation, not required computer responses.

**Alex:** I'm Alex. I coordinate a community garden, and I'd like to make it easier for volunteers to share the work.

**Sam:** I'm Sam. I help build small tools for community groups.

**Alex:** People keep asking who's watering, what needs harvesting, and whether the gate code has changed. Right now it's all buried in a group chat.

**Sam:** What if each garden had a simple shared board: today's jobs, who's taking each one, and a place for quick updates?

**Alex:** I like that better than another chat. For our first test, let's keep it to one garden and a dozen volunteers. We can run it for two weeks and see whether the jobs actually get done.

**Sam:** A practical question: how would a volunteer know a job is theirs? We could let them tap a button to claim it.

**Alex:** Yes, and then mark it done. I don't think we need accounts for the first test; we can use first names on a private shared link.

**Sam:** We should be careful about the gate code, though. A private link might get forwarded.

**Alex:** Agreed. Let's leave the code out of the tool and keep sharing it the way we do today.

**Sam:** Do volunteers actually want to check a board, or will they just wait for a message? That's probably our biggest assumption.

**Alex:** We could ask a few volunteers before building anything. Let's park that for a moment and think about the first screen.

**Sam:** The first screen could show three sections: needs doing, claimed, and finished. Maybe a photo of the garden at the top.

**Alex:** I think the photo is optional. The important thing is that it works on a phone and doesn't make people install anything.

**Sam:** We also need to decide how people hear about new jobs. We should look at how other volunteer coordination tools handle notifications.

*[Pause briefly; continue naturally if Sotto doesn't offer to help.]*

**Alex:** Sotto, can you find a couple of existing volunteer coordination tools and tell us how they notify volunteers? Please include sources.

*[Allow time for research and an answer; discuss what is useful or uncertain.]*

**Sam:** For our pilot, maybe we don't need to build notifications at all. A coordinator could post the board link in the existing group chat once a day.

**Alex:** That's a good experiment. We could count how many people claim a job after each post, and ask whether the reminder was useful.

**Sam:** Let's launch the pilot across all six gardens next Monday. That should give us a lot of feedback quickly.

*[Pause and give Sotto room to contribute. Discuss any response naturally.]*

**Alex:** Before we wrap up, who's going to speak with volunteers about whether they'd use the board?

**Sam:** I'll talk to four volunteers this week. I'll ask how they currently hear about jobs and show them a paper sketch, without telling them what answer we're hoping for.

**Alex:** Great. I'll write down the pilot jobs and check with the garden coordinator. Let's decide whether to build anything after those conversations.

## Facilitator notes

- Opportunities: introduction and names; brainstorming; an assumption raised and later revisited; a research hook followed by an explicit sourced request; a conflict between the one-garden pilot and a six-garden launch; decisions and owners.
- The research hook tests whether Sotto can notice a useful lookup. The direct request tests on-demand research even if it stayed quiet earlier. If Sotto has already supplied the information, adapt or skip the direct request.
- Sam and Alex resolve the gate-code concern themselves. Observe whether Sotto avoids belatedly raising it as if it were still open. The later volunteer question is also answered by a participant: an immediate interruption is not the desired outcome.
